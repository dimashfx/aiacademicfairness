Research proposal

Generative AI Use and Perceptions of Academic Fairness

Undergraduate students at Nazarbayev University, Fall 2026

Dinmukhamed Marat
PLS210 Political Science Research Methods

Some students use generative AI often for their coursework, and others rarely use it. This study asks whether that difference is linked to how fair students think AI-assisted academic work is. It uses a positivist approach: measure the variables, then test one hypothesis.

Research question

The whole study tries to answer this one question.

How does the use of generative AI affect undergraduate students’ perceptions of academic fairness at Nazarbayev University in Fall 2026?

Population
Undergraduate students
Context and location
Nazarbayev University, Astana, Kazakhstan
Time period
Fall 2026 semester

Why it is empirical and answerable

Observable

Students can report how often they use AI and what they think about it.

Measurable

Use and fairness views can be turned into numbers, for example with a student survey.

Testable

H1 predicts a pattern. The data can support it or not.

Limited in scope

One university, undergraduates only, one semester.

Empirical, not moral. The question does not ask whether AI use is right or wrong. It asks what students perceive as fair, and perceptions can be measured.

A careful point. Comparing students can show that AI use and fairness views go together. It does not prove by itself that use causes the difference.

Epistemological positioning

This study is positivist.

What positivism means

  • Knowledge comes from what we can observe and measure.
  • The social world has patterns that we can study with data.
  • The researcher stays neutral and tests ideas (hypotheses) against evidence.
  • The goal is patterns that go beyond one person’s story.

Why it fits this study

  • Both the IV and the DV can be measured.
  • H1 is a clear statement that data can support or reject.
  • Many students can be compared with the same questions.
  • The question is about a relationship between two variables, not about personal stories.

Contribution

What existing research shows

  • Many students already use GenAI, and their views are mixed.
  • Students disapprove of fully AI-written work but are unsure about smaller uses.
  • Students want clear rules.

Johnston et al., 2024; Chan, 2025; Farinosi & Melchior, 2025

What is missing

  • Few tests of whether more AI use goes with different fairness views.
  • Fairness is often explored in small qualitative studies.
  • Limited evidence from Kazakhstan.

What this study adds

  • A test of the link between AI use and fairness views.
  • A clear mechanism that can be examined.
  • Evidence from one Kazakhstani university that could inform AI policy.

Literature review, theme 1 of 4

Students use GenAI, and their views are mixed

Johnston et al. (2024)

Survey of 2,555 students at the University of Liverpool. Only 7% had not heard of any GenAI tool, and over half had used or considered using them for academic purposes.

Farinosi and Melchior (2025)

Survey of 1,366 students from 24 Italian higher education institutions. It covered use, ethical views, and expectations about policy. Male students were more likely than female students to use GenAI.

What this means for my studyUse differs from student to student. This is why use can work as a variable (the IV).

Literature review, theme 2 of 4

Where is the line between help and cheating?

Chan (2025)

Survey of 393 undergraduate and postgraduate students about “AI-giarism” scenarios. Students clearly disapproved of directly generating content with AI, but their views on subtler uses were ambivalent. The paper argues that traditional ideas of misconduct need to adapt.

Johnston et al. (2024)

54.1% supported tools like Grammarly, but 70.4% were unsupportive or somewhat unsupportive of using ChatGPT to write a whole essay. The authors conclude that students need clear policies.

What this means for my studyThe line between help and misconduct is unclear for students in these studies. This fits my mechanism idea, but these studies do not test it.

Literature review, theme 3 of 4

Ethical use and fairness

O’Dea et al. (2025)

Qualitative study of student views on ethical and equitable GenAI use in assessment at UK universities. The authors point to a lack of evidence on what ethical and equitable use means to students.

Delaney et al. (2025)

Interviews with 36 undergraduates. Students made their own judgments about AI fairness, even when school policies existed, and used AI inconsistently on assignments.

Johnston et al. (2024)

The authors say universities should make sure different groups of students have equal access to GenAI tools.

What this means for my studyThese fairness studies use interviews or other qualitative methods. They give rich detail, but they are not designed to test a pattern across many students.

Literature review, theme 4 of 4

The Kazakhstan context

Zhunusbekova and Askarkyzy (2025)

Survey of 840 undergraduates at three Kazakhstani universities in May 2024.

  • 65% used AI tools at least weekly for academic purposes.
  • 57% saw no significant conflict between AI use and academic integrity.
  • 96% wanted clear institutional policies.

What this means for my studyAI use is common among undergraduates in Kazakhstan. According to its abstract, this study focuses on academic integrity, not on fairness.

Literature review

The research gap

  • Studies describe how students use GenAI and what they think about it (Johnston et al., 2024; Farinosi & Melchior, 2025; Zhunusbekova & Askarkyzy, 2025).
  • Studies of misconduct and fairness show unclear and mixed views (Chan, 2025; Delaney et al., 2025; O’Dea et al., 2025).
  • From the abstracts, none of the six makes this its main test: do students who use GenAI more often judge AI-related practices as more fair?
  • Only one of the six is from Kazakhstan, and I have not confirmed that it includes NU.

This gap leads to the research question: How does the use of generative AI affect undergraduate students’ perceptions of academic fairness at Nazarbayev University in Fall 2026?

Theory and Hypotheses

How the study links AI use to fairness perceptions

Independent variable (IV)

Frequency and intensity of GenAI use

How often and how heavily students use GenAI for academic work

Mechanism

Changing understandings of legitimate academic work

What students believe counts as honest, acceptable work

Dependent variable (DV)

Perceptions of academic fairness

Whether AI-assisted work, assessment, and levels of AI use seem fair

Expected direction: more use, more permissive views.

Theory and Hypotheses

Key concepts

IV: frequency and intensity of GenAI use

How often, and how heavily, a student uses generative AI for academic work.

Mechanism: changing understandings of legitimate academic work

What a student believes counts as honest, acceptable work. Using GenAI shows students many kinds of AI-assisted work, and this can shift that belief.

DV: perceptions of academic fairness

Whether students think AI-assisted work, assessment, and different levels of AI use are fair.

Why the mechanism connects the twoMore use means more contact with AI-assisted work. More contact can widen what a student sees as legitimate. A wider view of legitimate work makes AI-assisted work look more acceptable and fair.

Theory and Hypotheses

Hypothesis

H1: More frequent use of generative AI for academic work is associated with more permissive perceptions of AI-related academic practices and fairness.

In plain words: students who use AI more often are more likely to see AI-assisted work as acceptable and fair.

Supports H1

Students with higher AI use also give more permissive fairness answers.

Does not support H1

No pattern, or the opposite pattern: more use with less permissive views.
